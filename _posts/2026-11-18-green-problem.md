I've been reading a couple books on painting by the illustrator [James Gurney](https://jamesgurney.com/) and when I came to this page in *Color and Light* titled "The Green Problem," I was like, hey, it's me!

{% include image.html url="/assets/images/GurneyGreenProblem.jpeg" caption="" alt="page in book with heading the green problem" %}

Me figuratively as explained in my last post, but also me as an artist who loves green. This section in the book talks about the fundamental importance of the color green and the sensitivity of the human eye to green, but also how it's not attractive. I mean attractive literally, like it's just too chill of a color to draw the eye, which made me think of [Go Away Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Go_Away_Green), which always makes me think of how [red is the most universally identified color across cultures and languages](https://nautil.us/why-red-means-red-in-almost-every-language-235557). Green and red!

My favorite thing about living in Missouri is the lush green, and I've long been wanting to create images of foliage (especially creeks, my fave), and wanting to create them in gouache, which is opaque watercolor. My interest in gouache comes from its use in a major artistic interest for me: animation background paintings. 

I love the look of cel animation, which is the traditional animation process of animating using clear celluloid (cels). The cels have the characters on them, and they're clear so that the setting can show through, established by the static background painting underneath. I love background paintings and you can bet this is far from the last time I'll be writing about them.

{% include image.html url="/assets/images/CelAnimationDiagram.png" caption="I stole this from Wikipedia" alt="diagram showing traditional animation cel setup" %}

Aaanyway, I took a class last year on gouache for beginners and was surprised by how much I like working with it considering my distaste for painting with watercolor (I love how watercolor can look, I just can't make it look like that). The ability to reactivate on both paper and palette is oil-like without the whole song and dance of oils.

When I went back this summer to apply that knowledge to foliage, I hit a bit of a wall. I was getting too bogged down in all the detail, all those individual leaves. So I did some studies, first a value (light and shadow) study and then a color study:

{% include image.html url="/assets/images/SketchbookTreeValueStudy.jpeg" caption="When the rainbow shrimp tape didn't hold I had to go to the boneless stickers" alt="pencil thumbnail and gouache black and white sketch of foliage, taped with orange boneless butcher stickers" %}

{% include image.html url="/assets/images/SketchbookTreeColorStudy.jpeg" caption="" alt="simple gouache sketch of foliage" %}

I was happy with this result. I also drew a fair bit of foliage in ink this summer and made a lot of technical progress on understanding the relationship between texture and value. I did this little painting from a photo I took in the parking lot at the pool where I lifeguard:

{% include image.html url="/assets/images/YParkingLot.jpeg" caption="" alt="painting of trees with grass in foreground" %}

I was really happy with this one! It successfully captures the harsh high summer feel I wanted and it's very background-y. I was feeling confident so I decided to go on to a more ambitious paint. Spoiler, that one would fail. I set myself up for failure first by choosing a bad reference photo:

{% include image.html url="/assets/images/TGPReference.jpeg" caption="" alt="small stream surrounded by lush vegetation" %}

As I said, I like lush, so I like this picture I took in the park by my house. But this is objectively not a good photo to work from because the values are scattered, as in all the lights and darks are mixed up rather than having large, defined areas of shadow. This makes it visually confusing. 

The second way I set myself up for failure was going too big. You don't see a lot of large gouache paintings and now I know why! The fast dry time was so hard to work with. Here's how that ended:

{% include image.html url="/assets/images/BigGouache.jpeg" caption="Featuring Dewey" alt="large unfinished gouache painting of stream with cat peeking out behind" %}

I got pretty discouraged by this, to the point that I became determined to create a painting from the same photo even though I knew it was bad. I got some higher quality gouache, changed the composition, and went smaller. Even so, I really wanted to quit midway through. Andy pushed me to stick with it and in the end I feel like it was a success:

{% include image.html url="/assets/images/TGPPainting.jpg" caption="Something #1" alt="finished gouache painting of stream" %}

It's not perfect. I'm happy with the foreground and sense of light, but it doesn't match the darker background. This is because I changed my approach halfway through; I think it could be adjusted but I'm just over it for real now. Good enough!

And with that, my first something is in the books. This described a months-long saga and I have like two weeks a pop for my remaining 23 somethings, so I don't expect future posts to be like this one. I'll try to take more work-in-progress photos of individual pieces to give you a sense of the process.

I'm not sure what to do for #2. I have a few ideas and also it's Christmas present making season, so a couple somethings may be getting taken up by that. I'm going to try to follow my interests...and write shorter posts.
