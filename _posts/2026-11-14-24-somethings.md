## Red and Green Gods  

When my dad died, I inherited his drugs. This was mostly a big bag of the awful ditchweed he smoked but also included a small greenish mushroom chocolate bar. This was a surprise given I’d never known my dad to venture into hallucinogens, and indeed there were only a couple squares gone. Still in the raw space of my new grief, the bar felt like a parting gift.

I’d tried mushrooms once before with a more-experienced friend, under a ring of old trees in the park. It was a lovely afternoon, but not a life-changing trip dose. I tend towards being cautious and practical, so when I decided to take the bar a couple months after his death, I carefully measured and ate a similarly conservative dose. I bundled up, went down my apartment stairs to head for the park, set one foot out the door, turned around, went back up the stairs, and ate the entire rest of the bar. And then I went back, alone, to those old trees in the park to see what would happen.

Nothing at first, and then a wave of so much something that I began to panic, an instinct rising to tighten the reins. In my head I heard the voice of the friend I’d taken mushrooms with before, saying what she would no doubt have told me had she been there: surrender. I laid down on the yellow winter grass and looked up at the sky, ringed by swaying, naked branches.

Then I heard them—those branches, the trees, the wind, spirits? Gods. They chastised me for always trying to be in control, for trying to be a god myself. Utterly humbled, I supplicated. I journeyed home, threw up, sobbed in the bath, sobbed to my cat, sobbed as I watched the sun set.

I have a true favorite color and it is green, specifically the green of mid-spring leaves on my favorite tree (silver maple) just after it rains. Green is the color of life! I wanted all green all the time. But when my trip ended, I understood that green is nothing without its complement: red. Red is death, change, breaking where green holds steady. It was time for me to get to know red. Not just get to know—I started praying to red, to the red and green gods, whatever they may be. I still do.

I don’t usually share this with people, because it sounds completely insane. But thank those gods it happened because that strange new faith carried me through harder times the next couple of years, years where at times I felt I had already died but was somehow still waking up each day by mistake. By the end I was completely spent. I decided I had to change my life and I quit my (quite good) job to take 2026 as sort of adult gap year, space to find out what I wanted.

## Dreams

It’s been almost a year since that last day at the office. It’s been a great year, probably the best. It took me about the first three months to learn to just rest, and then I learned to let myself have fun. I spent countless hours in the sun as a lifeguard, watched babies grow up, cleaned my whole house, played my first round of golf, cooked many meals, voraciously read dumb novels, rode a new surfboard, etc. Between it all laid in my bed and on the couch and on picnic blankets doing glorious, precious nothing.

When I was planning this year, a mentor of mine told me he thought I’d be bored within a week. I said it would take me a year. There was a point this summer where I thought I would never again recover my sense of ambition, my drive to do hard things. But by the end of September, I had started to feel an itch and by the end of October, I was scratching it raw. It was time to learn how to *do*.

So what had I learned about what I want to do? I feel blessed that my problem is always too many dreams, not enough. I’m simplifying, but I had narrowed it down to about four:

1. **Be a student**: Go to grad school for anthropology or something adjacent as a continuation of my undergrad, which I loved.
    
2. **Be a teacher**: Get some experience working in education, probably with kids, maybe teaching English or even Montessori training.
    
3. **Be a surfer**: Move somewhere with a decent break where I can surf every day and progress.
    
4. **Be an artist**: Draw, paint, write, and develop my earliest passion of telling stories through words and images.

You may be thinking that there’s a life that integrates all four of these. I haven’t given up on that vision, but maybe I don’t have quite that much time. The thing is, I just don’t know how much time I really have. So which one do I pick as my priority? My process of elimination was to imagine that it was the last day of my life, to imagine how it would feel to know that time was up and all I had was the past. 

My academic life I could live with, knowing I missed thrilling intellectual connections but also many stressful hours under artificial lights staring into an artificial light. The same with education; the call is strong but my memories with the children in my life fill me with enough warmth to drive away any true regret. Surfing is a bit sadder, such a pure love and so little time together, but I feel lucky to have found it at all and I cherish my handful of time-stopping waves.

Oh, and then there is art. An unspeakable, choking despair I can’t even turn to look at. The thought of facing death having only made what I’ve already made sparked in me a physical panic, the kind of fear that claws you back to life or, in failing, traps your soul as a ghost.

This has been an unfortunate realization, because this is the hard dream. This is the one I’m not sure I can do. Making things is what I love most of all, but I’ve also been running from it almost my whole life. I’m scared to make things, to see them and know that they’re bad, for others to see them and know that I’m bad, that I’m nothing. I’ve often felt like no amount of logic can refute this fear.

On a practical level I knew what I needed was a project, a framework to push myself through the fear. I didn’t think about where I should start or how; I thought about how I might make it easy. Is it such a crime to want it to be easy? My gods could help me, I thought. They could save me! I got more mushrooms, this time from a friend who wouldn’t let me pay for them, warning me they might be old. I took them all, a high dose by weight, and I went back to the old trees in the park and I laid down and waited. And waited.

The mushrooms were too old. Nothing happened, and nothing needed to—I didn’t have to trip to feel the gods’ rebuke. Here I was, back to insult them with my insolence, a cheater looking for a shortcut. I tried to sketch the branches, got frustrated, and gave up. I decided then that if I really wanted to, I could give up on the whole art thing forever. Maybe it’s really just too hard, and besides, I have three good dreams left!

I laid there for a long time. I decided I don’t want to give up. This is how I’m starting.

## The Project

My first thought for a project was to basically repeat AP Art, which I took my senior year of high school. This came to mind because I was looking for something that would be like school and involve making a lot of work, and because I was talking about AP Art with one of my teen lifeguard coworkers who's in it (and a talented artist), and also because this year I had a dream about it. 

In the dream, I was talking to an artist who had just published a graphic novel. I was gushing about how much I loved it, and then some people came and said it had gotten bad reviews. I told the artist she shouldn't worry, that they were just grading her on the wrong criteria, and that that happened to me in AP Art and it made me quit drawing for 10 years. Then I woke up and was like, is that true?

Not really, but maybe a little. Without going too into the weeds, the AP Art exam is a 24-piece portfolio with two sections. One is the "breadth" section of 12 images that show a range of skill. Here's my favorite piece from that section of my AP Art portfolio:

I got a 5/5 on that section. The other section is "depth" or concentration, which is 12 images that show a sustained exploration of an idea. I ended up doing semi-abstract drawings of my old house from memory. To this day, my concentration was probably the most positive art-making process of my life. Here's one of my favorites:


{% include image.html url="/assets/images/highschoolbasement.jpg" alt="abstract pastel drawing of a basement" %}

I got a 3/5 on this section. A passing grade. In an email thread from 2013, my art teacher wrote me about favorite students who got 3s and went on to be professional artists. I wrote that I was a little disappointed but not devastated. I wonder if I might have internalized it more than I realized. But I also wrote "I'm pretty sure I couldn't give up art if I wanted to."

I think the dream for me would be to combine these two images: creating from my imagination with my skill level of creating from observation. But I'm getting ahead of myself, because first I have to create, period. After some consideration, I decided following the AP Art criteria and timeline would be both limiting and unsustainable. 

Here is my current goal:
- **Make 24...somethings**. I don't want to limit myself on this, so it could be finished pieces, completed steps in larger projects, sketchbook explorations, failed experiments. I trust my heart to know what counts as a something.
- **Share the process**. I plan to post here whether things go well or poorly with the focus on learning. At some point in 2027, I also plan to do a little in-person show for local friends and family.
- **Finish by the end of 2027**. Will I succeed at that? I'm a little doubtful, but it'd be cool!

Well, that is long enough for this 2000-word post. I don't know what's going to happen, but I hope I'll learn something. Yes, I'm still afraid that we'll all see my work and know that it's bad and that I'm bad and I'm nothing, but maybe that wouldn't be so bad and maybe [it just doesn't matter](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=necpWHvXwKA){:target="_blank"}. Wish me luck, and I hope you'll come along.
